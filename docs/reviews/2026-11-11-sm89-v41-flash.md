# SM89 fork review: DeepSeek V4.1 Flash on 2× RTX 4090 D 48 GB

Review of `hughmadden/cuteafd@379b26792` (claude/sm89-up3, 134 commits on upstream `c64956704`) and `hughmadden/sparkinfer-glmrt@ce9aec54` (10 commits on upstream `78cfee4e`), dated 2026-11-11. It is based on code reading, the fork's own commit measurements and published research. No engine, GPU or Spark was run. File references are to the `379b26792` tree; SparkInfer paths are under `third_party/sparkinfer/`.

## Bottom line

The fork's SM89 bring-up is careful and well measured:

- the big decode GEMVs run at 92–96% of the card's read ceiling;
- the KV formats match the official recipe bit for bit;
- the expert path moved to exact F16 MMA.

What is left is mostly structural. On this rig a C1 verify step is about 60% Spark expert reads and 30% single-GPU attention work. At C1, one 4090 D sits idle while the other runs each layer.

- **Biggest engine-side gain:** a head-split attention block built on the fork's new device-flag pushes, about **+6.5–8% C1 decode**. Several smaller items add 6–9% more.
- **Accuracy:** the dense FP8 projections still use the lossy Ada FP8 MMA that the expert path already dropped.
- **Biggest single lever overall:** a deployment choice, not code. TP6 over six Sparks is about **+15–20% C1** by the model.

| | |
|---|---|
| Modelled C1 verify step | ≈30 ms (Sparks 18.2, GPU attention side 8.8, draft 2.4, head 0.7) |
| Engine-side gains (C1 code) | ≈+12–17% (A1–A6 at 32–128K, before D1's ~0.7% cost) |
| KV per token (global, HBM) | 890 B, already at the recipe's FP4/FP8 floor |
| Accuracy items | 1: dense FP8 GEMVs, Ada in-block MMA precision |

## 3. Decode findings (GPU side)

### A1. Head-split attention on device flags (largest engine-side item)

**Evidence.**
- TP2 attention, query and output projections are all `off` for V4.1 (`scripts/lib/release-common.sh:1177-1179`).
- The existing opt-in paths synchronize through cross-device events (`v41_projection_tp2.rs:341-362`).
- Enabling any of them makes verify passes host-drained and skips the plan-equals-runtime admission check (`v41_target_pass/distributed.rs:414-421`, `v41_native_serve/distributed.rs:343-348`).
- PLAN records why they lost on the PRO 6000 in October: "each projection there ends in a cross-device event and a host wait, so a gain needs V4.1's per-layer flow rebuilt around device-side flags" (`PLAN.md:455-470`).
- The fork now has exactly those primitives:
  - producer-stream pushes with device flags for A1–A9 (3c0cf36a5);
  - one system-scope release per push (8b4ee1c32): a 12 KiB hop takes 3.4 µs, 96 KiB 6.4 µs, and a two-way 96 KiB exchange 6.7 µs.

**Why it pays more here than on the PRO 6000.**
- The head-splittable work (q_b, sparse core, o_a, o_b) is ~153 µs per layer on a 4090 D, against ~115 µs measured on the PRO 6000. Hops cost about the same on both.
- The fork has measured half-width kernels: q_b_tp2 at ~890 GB/s, o_b_tp2 at ~868 GB/s, and a 32-head core at ~20 µs. With those, the split saves 21.4 + 4.8 + 17.7 + 22.1 = **66 µs per layer** before hops.
- Hops cost 8–12 µs per layer, and replicated KV and small weights cost ~0.3 ms of tier per step. The net is **1.9–2.2 ms per step, +6.5–8% C1**.
- A token-split latent (PLAN item 11, `attention=context`) avoids the replication loss.

**Shape that stays bit-exact.**
- **Inputs:** both GPUs carry the residual and run mHC, q_a and kv redundantly, so the inputs need no hop.
- **q_b:** split by heads. It is a column split, so per-column arithmetic is unchanged, and q_b_tp2 is already tested bit-for-bit.
- **Sparse core:** 32 heads per GPU, since heads are independent.
- **o_a:** split by its 8 groups.
- **o_b:** the existing N-split `o_b_tp2` on the fixed decode recipe, bit-exact per row up to 80 rows (a55b5e9c). It needs one exchange of the o_a output (≈25 KB each way at 6 rows) and an all-gather of the o_b halves (≈15 KB each way).
- **FFN result:** it is already pushed once per layer (A7); under the split it is broadcast instead.
- **KV placement:** the CSA2 groups (`v41_backbone_cache/placement.rs:9-12`) constrain only where KV lives, not head ownership.

**Costs and risks.**
- **Memory:** replicated KV costs ~1.8 GB at the 2M pool, since the encoder's 534 B and the decoder's 356 B per token are each copied to the other card. Each GPU also gains the other half's replicated small weights (q_a, kv, index, mHC fn, router), ~0.4 GB. Together that is ~1.3 GB per GPU, or about 140 fewer tier experts (0.9% of routed bytes, 0.2–0.3 ms per step).
- **Prefill:** keep the layer split. At 2,752-row units, the o_a and o_b exchanges (~40 MB per layer unit) cost about what they save.
- **C16:** little gain, because the Sparks dominate there.
- **dSpark:** the same split applies to its three blocks, about 380 MB of attention weights per draft and all on GPU1 today. That is worth ~0.2 ms more.

### A2. Engram kv projection off the critical path

**Evidence.**
- `v41_engram/layer.rs:305-330` launches the FP8 GEMM, then the gate, both after `chain::join` on the residual. The GEMM maps embeddings 6144 → 25600 (four per-stream keys plus one value).
- The gate kernel (`native/families/deepseek_v41/cuda/engram.cu`) is the only part that reads the hidden state.
- The projection depends only on the gathered n-gram rows, which are determined by the tokens.
- This matches the paper's intent that the "first module's prefetch overlaps computation in the first Transformer block".

**Change.**
- Queue the projection on the Engram load stream at low stream priority, once the rows land. A natural slot is while GPU0 waits on layer 0's Spark wave, about 400 µs of idle time.
- Layer 14's projection can run in any earlier wait window.
- Join before the gate.

**Estimate.** The projection reads 157 MB at ~890 GB/s, ~175 µs per Engram layer, so 0.35 ms per step (+1.2% C1). In prefill, the 2,752-row GEMM takes ~2.5 ms per Engram layer per unit and can hide in Spark waits (~1%).

### A3. Index scoring at long context

**Evidence.**
- The fork's own timing puts selection at 0.37 ms per C1 verify step at 32K and 1.03 ms at 128K (2e7dee4e8). It grows linearly with context: layer 20 alone goes from 94 µs to 401 µs.
- The AOT scorer (`third_party/sparkinfer/b12x/attention/dsa_indexer/_v41_overlay.py:37,105`) launches `grid=(width/64, rows)`.
  - Each warp scores 16 candidates for one row and re-decodes that row's FP4 Q from global memory for every tile.
  - Every row re-reads the same keys and page-table entries.
- On the Full layers (2, 8, 14, 20), all rows of one request score the same causal candidate set.

**Change.**
- Make the verify rows of one request extra MMA N columns. A warp loads and decodes 16 keys once and multiplies them against 6 × 32 head columns.
- Stage Q once per CTA in shared memory.
- Keep the per-head fold (BF16 round, ReLU, weight, the same head-sum tree) so scores stay bit-identical.
- Fuse the per-tile top-k carry into fewer launches.

**Estimate.** 2–3× on Full layers:

| Context | Saved per step | C1 gain |
|---|---:|---:|
| 32K | 0.13 ms | +0.4% |
| 128K | 0.5 ms | +1.7% |
| 512K–1M | 2–4 ms | +6–10% |

This matters for long agentic sessions. For scale, SGLang's V4-Flash single-batch decode on H200 loses ~10% going from 4K to 900K; on this slower card with this scorer, the share at 1M would be larger.

For the top-k half, TensorRT-LLM's Guess-Verify-Refine seeds selection with the previous step's set (~44% overlap) and stays exact: 22.7 µs against 43.7 µs for radix select at K=2048. It is an option for the 16K-candidate Reindex calls once scoring is fixed.

### A4. Sparse attention core

**Evidence** (`native/families/deepseek_v41/cuda/v41_sparse_attention.cu`).
- Compressed layers split 640 keys (128 window + 512 selected) into 10 parts, each one 64-key tile.
- Every (row, part) writes an FP32 partial of 64 heads × 514 floats (131.6 KB, line 132). A separate `merge` launch reads them back (line 439).
- On sm_89 the 98 KB tile admits one block per SM.
- At 6 rows the call takes 24.8 µs, against a roofline of ~3–4 µs (≈1.3 MB of KV, 0.5 GFLOP).
- In Reuse layers the main KV and top-k come from the source layer, so 8 of the 10 decoded tiles are identical across the group's layers.

**Change.**
1. The last-arriving block per row merges the parts, in fixed part order. The partials stay in L2 and one launch goes away.
2. Use 32-key tiles at ≤6 rows, for two blocks per SM.
3. Keep decoded BF16 selected-key tiles per (row, part) for a CSA2 group (3 MB at 6 rows, L2-resident), and skip the FP4 gather in the group's 3–5 Reuse layers.

**Estimate.** ~10 µs per layer at C1 (+1–1.5%). At C16 (48-row lanes, 124 µs per call) it is +3–5%.

**Prior art.**
- FlashMLA's FP8 sparse decode on Hopper is limited by dequantisation: about 50 CUDA-core cycles per token against about 34 MMA cycles at 64 heads per CTA. That is why skipping the FP4 gather and decode in Reuse layers is worth more than its bytes suggest.
- LeanAttention merges split partials in the same launch, 1.27× over FlashDecoding on average.
- FlashDecoding++ removes the synchronised rescale, 1.37×.
- FlashMLA overlaps its combine with PDL. sm_89 has no PDL, so the in-kernel merge matters more here.
- No upstream sparse-MLA decode kernel targets sm_89 (vLLM lists 9.x/10.x/12.x only), so this kernel stays the fork's own.

### A5. Kernel boundaries on sm_89

Ada has no programmatic dependent launch: PDL needs sm_90, and the SparkInfer kernels that use it are SM90+ paths. So every kernel boundary is a full drain and ramp, even inside graphs.

The query side alone issues frequencies, q_a, norm, q_b and RoPE as separate launches (`v41_attention_query.rs:329-388`). The official stack runs a Reuse layer in 11 decode kernels.

Cheap wins, together about +0.5–1%:
1. One GEMV for `q_a`+`kv`, which share their input: 9.2 MB at ~880 GB/s, against 702 and 574 GB/s separately. That saves ~3.8 µs per layer.
2. The router's skinny route runs at 21–46% of roofline at 16–64 rows (16d88384), costing ~13 µs per layer per C16 lane.
3. Fold the frequency computation into RoPE.

### A6. Per-layer eager copies on the critical streams

**Evidence.** A verify pass launches about 4 graphs per layer (~23 nodes). Code reading (not a trace) estimates another 30–36 eager operations per layer on top, about 1,300–1,450 per pass. Many of them are small copies queued in stream order between graphs:

- an H2D of positions before the query graph (`v41_attention_query.rs:564`);
- in sparse attention, a D2D of the rotated query (393 KB at 6 rows), plus H2Ds of descriptors, metadata and replay begins (`v41_sparse_attention.rs:412, 680-713`);
- in the output tail, a D2D of the attention values plus an H2D of positions (`v41_attention_output.rs:517-518`);
- a D2D of the router input plus an H2D of the mask (`v41_backbone_router.rs:769-770`);
- the SWA-window D2D and H2D, and the D2D of the next layer's inputs (`v41_block.rs:507, 600`).

That is roughly ten copies per layer. The host is ahead, so they cost nothing on the CPU. On the GPU timeline, though, each small copy costs its copy-engine latency (≈2–3 µs, more if the engine is busy with staging).

**Change.**
- **Quick first step.** The sparse-attention uploads are queued after `chain::join`, so they wait for the query graph even though they don't depend on it. Queue them before the join, on the same stream: they then overlap the producer and stay ordered after the previous layer's reads.
- **Fuller fix.** The per-layer host metadata is known before the pass starts. Build it into one pinned per-pass table, upload it once, and have each layer's graph read its slice.
- **Activations.** Point producers at the consumers' fixed graph inputs, so activations need no D2D. The existing graph-pointer lifetime rule still holds, because both ends are captured with fixed addresses.

**Estimate.** 20–35 µs per layer, so 0.8–1.4 ms per step (+2–4%). The nsys trace in section 12 shows these as memcpy rows, which makes them easy to confirm first.

**Same family, at commit.** The batched multi-layer window store (`cuteafd_v41_kv_store_layers`, up to 64 layers in one launch) is enabled only with one RTX (`cuteafd-loader/src/plan/layout/v41.rs:57`: `window_batch = ranks == 1 && …`). On the pair, every verify commit issues 40 per-layer window stores plus 4 source commits (`v41_backbone_cache.rs:790-805`). One batched store per GPU saves ~40 launches per step (~0.1–0.15 ms, +0.4%).

## 4. Accuracy: dense FP8 projections on sm_89

**Finding.**
- `third_party/sparkinfer/b12x/_lib/sm89_fp8.py:6-9` calls each K32 step's `mma.m16n8k32` E4M3 MMA exact. It says results "differ from the SM120 block-scaled MMA only by FP32 accumulation order". The GEMV and the GEMM call it at lines 310 and 814–823.
- SparkInfer 3cd2737e then showed that this same instruction "sums a K32 block's 32 products with too few bits". With only one nonzero product per block the error vanishes, which isolates the in-block sum as the cause.
- The experts moved to two exact F16 `m16n8k16` per K32. BF16 outputs equal to the recipe went from 75.00% to 99.973%, and relL2 from 2.6e-3 to 2.5e-5.
- The dense projections (q_a, q_b, kv, o_a, o_b, index_q, shared expert, Engram, dSpark main) still use the lossy path.
- Their tests bound the error against FP64 at BF16-rounding size (max relative error 1.7–3.7e-3, 2b14646e). A bar that size cannot see a one-ulp flip in a quarter of the outputs. An equality-against-recipe check like 3cd2737e's can, and the dense path has never had one.

**Independent confirmation.**
- MMA-Sim's bit-accurate models (arXiv 2511.10909) show what Ada's FP8 QMMA does: it aligns the C input and every product to the largest exponent, keeps 13 fractional bits and truncates the rest. m16n8k32 runs as two chained 16-product dot-adds. Ada's FP16/BF16 HMMA keeps 24 bits.
- PyTorch's Feb 2026 study found the same e8m13 behaviour on RTX 40-series and H100.
- SageAttention2 calls the Ada/Hopper FP8 accumulator "FP22".
- The fork's "MMA into a zero partial, promote in FP32" already avoids truncating the running accumulator. Only the 32-product in-block sum remains lossy, and that is exactly what F16 widening removes.

**Change.**
- Use the same F16 widening in `Sm89Block32Gemv` for decode and verify. The GEMVs are bandwidth-bound, and the expert waves paid only 1.8–4.7% for the switch, so expect about 0.15–0.25 ms per C1 step.
- Keep FP8 for prefill unless the fidelity A/B shows a difference. Prefill is compute-bound, and F16 MMA runs at half the FP8 rate.
- Gate the change with the quick fidelity check (KL and top-1 against the BF16 root), one launch per arm.
- DeepSeek trains and serves on Hopper's limited FP8 accumulation, so expect only a small KL change.
- Either way, the docstring should say what the hardware actually does.

## 5. Spark side and system

### B1. Expert kernel bandwidth on GB10

- The tier model charges 21.25 µs of kernel per TP4 quarter-expert (4.56 MB), ~215 GB/s.
- Published GB10 measurements put sustained GPU weight reads at ~231 GB/s (85% of 273) for a dense decode workload, and up to ~238–262 GB/s on clean units. The 205–207 GB/s triad figure measures a different access pattern.
- So there is headroom, but it is bounded: 215 → 235 GB/s would save ~1.4 ms per C1 step (+5%).
- One ncu pass on a Spark over a decode wave is worth doing before any work. The usual suspects are the FC1 → FC2 dependency per expert and the route-plan prologue.

### B2. Tier capacity

- The uniform step model saves 3.54 ms per step at a 17.3% byte share, so each 1% of routed bytes moved onto the GPUs is worth ~0.2 ms. The census-ranked tier does better per byte at first and worse at the margin.
- One GiB per GPU holds ~117 TP2 halves (9,131,264 B each). That is about 0.76% of bytes, so +0.5%.
- The memory ledger (`cuteafd::memory` report) on the 4090 D pair shows what is reclaimable. Prefill reserves and verify-width workspaces are the usual candidates.
- The 2M-token pool is only ~1.8 GB of global KV (section 6).

### B3. Fixed 34 µs per layer

- 34 µs × 40 layers is 1.4 ms per step (5%).
- The path: SM staging into pinned host memory, a host-word poll by the transport worker, RDMA, the Spark, reply frames into host memory, then the GPU pull (16–24 µs at 1–6 rows, 0d556132e).
- NIC-written replies with a device flag would avoid the pull. This is PLAN's `CUTEAFD_SPARK_WRITE` write mode, built but never run.
- At 1–6 rows the reply is only 40–240 KiB, so even a modest GPUDirect write rate into 4090 D memory could beat a 16–24 µs pull. Measure that rate on this host first.
- This applies at decode widths only, and might save 5–10 µs per layer.

## 6. KV cache

- **Formats are at the recipe floor:**
  - main (compressed) KV is E2M1 with an E4M3 scale per 16 (`v41_kv.cu:30-38`), 288 B per entry;
  - SWA KV is E4M3 with a UE8M0 scale per 32 (lines 40–47), 528 B per entry;
  - the indexer is MXFP4, 68 B per entry.

  That is exactly the paper's choice ("E2M1 with one E4M3 scale per 16 channels", with SWA kept FP8 "due to its sensitivity to quantization"). FlashMLA's V4.1 kernels use the same 288 B and 528 B layouts. Going lower would change numerics, so there is nothing to do here.
- **Per token:** 1.5 encoder entries (ratio 2 at the Full layers 2, 8 and 14) plus 1 decoder entry (layer 20) give 2.5 × (288 + 68) = **890 B**, the paper's number. GPU0 holds 534 B per token and GPU1 356 B. The 2M-token pool is ~1.8 GB across both cards. KV is not the binding memory consumer on this pair; the tier is.
- **Under A1,** prefer the token-split latent (PLAN item 11) to replication, or accept ~1.8 GB of replication (~0.3 ms per step of tier).
- **Reuse layers** re-gather the same FP4 tiles; that is the KV-side half of A4.
- **Host prefix tier:** the auto quota is min(32 GiB, 25% RAM), about 38M tokens of global KV, which is ample.
- **SWA snapshots:** the exact SWA front costs ~2.7 MB per snapshot (40 × 128 × 528 B). The official deployment drops SWA from its persistent cache and replays it approximately ("not mathematically equivalent"). The engine's exact-restore rule correctly does not copy that approach.

## 7. Prefill

**Measured.** The fork reports 8K at 4,853–4,924 tok/s and 32K at 5,588 tok/s (643b6aa09). Everything included, that averages ~25–28 ms of wall time per 2,752-row encoder layer unit. The measured critical path is 16.4 ms per layer unit.

**Resource estimates per layer unit:**

| Resource | Estimate |
|---|---:|
| GPU0 (dense GEMMs ~2.2 ms, attention ~2.3 ms, reply pull 4.2 ms) | ~9 ms |
| Spark weight reads (all 384 quarter-experts per layer, 1.75 GB per Spark) | ~8.5 ms |
| NIC, each way | ~4.7 ms |

All three sit well under the measured time, so prefill looks latency-chained rather than resource-bound. A prefill trace should come before any code. Candidates, in order:

1. **C1, reply ingest.** The four ranks' BF16 partials are 40 KiB per row: 107.5 MiB and 4.24 ms to land per layer unit (0d556132e). An exact Spark-side reduce-scatter, summing in rank order in FP32, halves those bytes and stays bit-identical to today's reducer. It was parked on the PRO 6000 rig at +3–12% (`PLAN.md:682-690`, branch `work/spark-reduce`). The 4090 D host's PCIe Gen4 path is new evidence in its favour: the copy engine runs at ~24 GB/s, but SM stores into host memory only manage 1.8–2.1 GB/s (ebc2dc9d7).
2. **Decoder bounded replay.** Replaying 128 rows through 20 decoder layers touches ~330 experts per layer, so it reads nearly the full expert weights: ~100–150 ms per request at TP4. That dominates TTFT for short agentic suffixes. The tier and TP6 shrink it in proportion; nothing else removes it without changing the recipe.
3. **Pipeline depth.** An 8K prompt is three units, so little overlap is possible. The gap between 32K and 8K suggests fill and fixed costs. Smaller units for short prompts trade GEMM efficiency for overlap, and are worth one A/B.
4. **GPU1 during prefill.** It runs only TP2 halves. The CSA2 groups allow GPU0 to own layers 0–7 and GPU1 layers 8–39, so units could pipeline across the cards. That only pays if the trace shows GPU0 is the bound, and the asymmetric weights cost tier bytes on GPU1.

## 8. Deployment lever (not engine code)

**F1. TP6 over six Sparks.**
- **Evidence:** on the PRO 6000 cards of 18 Oct, going from 2 RTX + 4 Sparks to 2 RTX + 6 Sparks took C1 code from 155.8 to 177.8 tok/s (+14%) and C16 from 522 to 562 (+7.5%).
- **On this pair:** the Sparks are a larger share of the step, and the model gives −5.1 ms per step (+20%). That assumes the expert kernel scales with 1/6 slices, and charges +12 µs per layer for two Sparks on 50G links.
- **Prefill caveat:** prefill would suffer on 50G ranks, at 27.5 MiB of replies per layer unit. It would need a decode-TP6 / prefill-TP4 split, or faster links.

## 9. Considered and not recommended

- **Micro-batch ping-pong at C1** (MegaScale-Infer style).
  - Splitting a 6-row verify into halves re-reads, on the Sparks, any expert both halves touch. At ~24 distinct experts per wave, that adds about 7 expert reads (~150 µs) per layer to hide ~200 µs of GPU work, plus a second fixed round trip per layer.
  - MegaScale-Infer's own conditions (attention time ≈ expert time, communication well under compute) don't hold here.
  - Two lanes at C≥2 already provide the overlap.
- **A dynamic GPU expert cache.** Every published win (ProMoE, ExpertFlow, HybriMoE and others) comes from PCIe offload, where a miss means a host upload. Here the Sparks serve a miss anyway, and with exact restores any resident change invalidates snapshots. Together with the fork's replay pricing (99b914486, −66% net for LFU/LRU), this rules it out.
- **FR-Spec-style draft vocabulary subset.** FR-Spec keeps 93.3% of acceptance length at a 32K subset. Here the draft head is ~0.35–0.7 ms of a ~30 ms step, so losing ~6–7% of accepted tokens costs more than the head saves.
- **Pre-drafting during verify** (PEARL, SSD). dSpark reads the target's layer 37–39 states at accepted positions, so no draft can start before the verify ends, and C1 has no second drafter device.
- **Cutting the drafter's top-3 to top-2**, and MoE-Spec-style verify-expert caps. The first is lossless but pays in acceptance; the second changes outputs.
- **Lower KV precision, or making the FP8/NVFP4 reply codec the default.** Both change numerics. The paper keeps SWA KV at FP8 for its sensitivity, and post-hoc FP4 KV costs real accuracy on reasoning (SGLang: DeepSeek-R1 AIME25 0.507 → 0.400).

## 10. Host-level checks worth ten minutes

- **SM stores into host memory.**
  - SM stores run at 1.8–2.1 GB/s while SM loads and copy-engine copies run at 24 GB/s (ebc2dc9d7); that gap is what forced copy-engine staging. A store-only slowdown points at posted-write ordering more than address translation.
  - The driver has a PCIe relaxed-ordering mode (`NVreg_EnablePCIERelaxedOrderingMode=1`, default off), with no published measurements, so this is a test, not a prediction. Re-run the fork's own staging probe (`cuteafd_staging_probe`) with it on.
  - Separately, NVIDIA's GPUDirect RDMA docs require the IOMMU off or in pass-through, and published DMA studies show 55–70% losses from IOTLB misses with 4 KB pages. Check the coordinator host's IOMMU mode before judging NIC-written replies (B3).
- **FP8 tensor rate on this card.**
  - SparkInfer's prefill tables use a 597 TF/s ceiling, and report 425–507 TF/s for plain FP8 and Triton.
  - Published figures put GeForce FP8 with FP32 accumulate at half the FP16-accumulate rate. The corrected whitepaper, as quoted on NVIDIA's forums, gives 330.3 TFLOPS for the 4090, which implies ~294 for the 4090 D at its rated boost. Forum measurements of cuBLASLt FP8 on a 4090 land at ~330–340.
  - The fork's numbers and these cannot both hold. Re-derive the FLOP count, or check which accumulator the "plain" reference uses, before using those tables for roofline decisions.
  - The rankings in this review use only the GB/s tables, which are unaffected.

## 11. Checked and already good

- Decode GEMVs: q_b 938, o_a 923, o_b 928 and engram 878 GB/s at one row, against a 978 GB/s read ceiling (dc768bd1). Split-K was correctly left off.
- Exact F16 expert MMA (3cd2737e): the right call, at a cost of 1.8–4.7% on memory-bound waves.
- A static, census-ranked tier rather than dynamic caches under exact restores: the fork's own pricing settles it.
- Fused mHC begin/post (0.98 → 0.56 ms per C1 step).
- The radix index top-k: bit-identical, 0.88 → 0.37 ms at 32K.
- Copy-engine staging only behind met waits, and one system-scope release per peer push.
- Offline sm_89 exports, and per-arch SM-count plans (114 and 128).
- The draft-width cost model is fitted online (`cuteafd-core/src/simple_draft.rs`), so it adapts to this rig's step costs.

## 12. Measurement plan before building

1. One nsys trace of a warmed C1 code verify step on the 4090 D pair. It should show per-layer GPU idle time, kernel and copy count, the gaps between them, and the Spark wait. That confirms or corrects section 2 and sizes A1, A5 and A6.
2. ncu on the sparse attention call (6 and 48 rows) and on the index scorer at 128K, to split the time into gather latency, MMA and partial traffic (A3, A4).
3. The quick fidelity A/B for D1 (FP8 against F16 GEMVs), one launch per arm.
4. One Spark-side ncu of a 24-expert decode wave (B1).
5. One prefill trace at 8K, to find whether the critical chain is reply landing, the Spark wave or GPU0 (section 7).
